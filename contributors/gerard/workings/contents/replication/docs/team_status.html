Home contents: where the analysis stands

Owner Gerard Murphy As at 20 October 2026 AI support Claude Code Stage Analysis done · modelling not started · no submission yet

In short

  • The contents data is understood, cleaned and joined. Four data-quality problems were found and fixed in the pipeline, and two of them also affect motor and buildings.
  • One field, customer_service_contacts, leaks the target. It must be excluded, and the other lines should check for the same problem.
  • The main cost drivers are flood exposure, sum insured, tenure, region and prior claims. A single Tweedie GLM did as well as separate per-peril models, and splitting by claim size made things worse.
  • A policy's own claim history predicts its future claims strongly. That points to credibility-based experience rating, which is the most promising next gain.
  • Everything can be reproduced from raw data in SQL, Python and R, with an audit log. Six decisions are waiting for sign-off before any model is built on them.

However, factors do act differently on small and large claims: flood zone 5 is 1.25× for claims under 1,000 and 20× for claims of 15,000 and over, and accidental damage cover is 2.9× against 1.06×. The next step is to capture this with interactions in one model. Keep the per-peril structure ready for Week 2, where a different peril mix would favour it. The gaps between A, B and D have not yet been tested for significance.

Older history predicts as strongly as last year's, so a one-step Markov chain is the wrong structure. The pattern fits a stable hidden risk level per policy, which credibility-weighted experience rating handles.

Measured against expected claims from earned premium, which reflects everything the current pricing knows, the effect remains: the current rates do not price own-claims history. A large claim (5k+) last year gives next-year cost A/E 3.35. 85% of 2025 policies are renewals with history.

Reproducibility and audit trail

A replication pack rebuilds every number above from the raw parquet files. The SQL is the single source for data preparation. Python and R each run that SQL and then fit the models independently, and a reconciliation script compares the two.

  • All 32 SQL evidence files are byte-identical between Python and R. One-way results match exactly. Model scores agree to four decimal places, and each model component's predictions agree within 0.05%.
  • The final reconciliation passes 100 of 100 checks. Coefficients agree to five decimal places, after R's GLMs were rewritten as a penalised IRLS solver. One flood level with almost no claims is flagged as a warning, with identical values in both languages.
  • Every run appends a record (input and code hashes, decision switches, results) to a run log. A narrative audit log (A01–A10) and the decision log sit alongside it.
  • The pack is on branch contents/gerard under workings/contents/replication/. Competition data and row-level outputs are excluded from version control.
